Thread Engineering

Precision geometry that converts rotation into controlled clamping force.

Threading in fasteners is a precision-engineered helical system designed to convert rotational input into controlled axial movement and clamping force.

Structural Control Through Thread Geometry

Threading governs load transfer, joint stability, repeatability, and performance across real-world operating conditions. Thread systems are designed to align with application-specific requirements, enabling reliability, structural integrity, and predictable fastening performance across every fastening system.

Drive Types

Engineered tool engagement for controlled assembly.

Drive types define how torque is applied, transferred, and controlled. The correct drive improves installation speed, tool grip, automation compatibility, serviceability, and resistance to head damage.

Purpose of Drive Geometry

A drive is more than a tool interface. It is the engineered contact point that controls how rotational force enters the fastener and how consistently preload can be generated.

  • Torque transfer efficiency
  • Tool grip and engagement
  • Cam-out and slip resistance
  • Assembly speed and repeatability
  • Accessibility in confined areas
  • Service and maintenance ease

Slotted Drive

A single straight slot, simple and widely recognized, but with limited engagement and higher slip risk.

Common Use

Light-duty assemblies, electrical components, restoration work, and low-torque fastening.

Simple Low Torque High Slip Risk

Phillips Drive

A cross-shaped recess that improves centering and supports faster general assembly.

Common Use

Sheet metal, appliances, electrical products, consumer products, and general machinery.

Fast Install Good Centering Cam-Out Tendency

Pozidriv Drive

An enhanced cross-drive with additional ribs for better tool engagement and reduced cam-out.

Common Use

Furniture, panels, industrial assembly, electrical fittings, and production fastening.

Better Grip Repeatable Correct Bit Needed

Hex Socket Drive

An internal hexagonal recess operated with an Allen key or hex bit for strong tool control.

Common Use

Socket head cap screws, set screws, machine tools, automotive, and precision equipment.

High Torque Compact Clean Appearance

Hexalobular / Six-Lobe Drive

A star-shaped internal profile designed for high torque transfer and low cam-out.

Common Use

Automotive, electronics, appliances, aerospace assemblies, and high-speed production lines.

Low Cam-Out Automation Friendly Reduced Tool Wear

External Hex Drive

A six-sided external head driven by a spanner, socket, or wrench for heavy-duty fastening.

Common Use

Hex bolts, structural fasteners, construction, machinery, oil & gas, and heavy equipment.

Heavy Duty Field Friendly Easy Inspection

Square Drive

A square recess that offers strong tool retention and reduced slipping compared to simple drives.

Common Use

Wood construction, decking, furniture, industrial assemblies, and special fastening systems.

Good Retention Reduced Slip Reliable

Combination Drive

A multi-tool drive form, such as slotted plus Phillips, used for service flexibility.

Common Use

Electrical fittings, access panels, hardware, maintenance-friendly assemblies, and light-duty products.

Flexible Service Friendly Moderate Torque

12-Point Drive

A high-engagement internal or external profile with twelve contact points for precision fastening.

Common Use

Aerospace, automotive engines, high-performance machinery, and compact wrenching areas.

High Torque Precision Compact Access

Security / Tamper-Resistant Drives

Controlled-access drive forms such as pin-in-hex, pin-in-six-lobe, spanner, one-way, and tri-wing.

Common Use

Public infrastructure, transport systems, electrical panels, safety equipment, and access covers.

Tamper Resistant Controlled Access Security Focused
Selection Factors

Choose the drive based on assembly reality.

The correct drive type depends on torque requirement, installation method, service access, appearance, security need, coating thickness, and production speed.

Drive Selection Guide

Higher torque applications need stronger engagement and lower cam-out risk. Automated assembly benefits from stable bit retention, while field maintenance benefits from commonly available tools.

Torque Requirement

Select stronger drive engagement for high preload and heavy-duty fastening.

Assembly Speed

Use automation-friendly drives for repeatable engagement in high-volume lines.

Accessibility

Confined spaces may require internal drives, compact heads, or reduced wrench clearance.

Serviceability

Maintenance applications benefit from durable geometry and commonly available tools.

Tamper Resistance

Public or restricted assemblies may require security drive systems.

Appearance

Visible assemblies may require clean, recessed, low-profile, or premium head forms.

Material & Coating

Drive geometry must remain functional after plating, coating, heat treatment, or finishing.

Repeatability

Production fastening requires consistent drive dimensions and stable tool engagement.

Failure Prevention

Prevent drive damage before assembly defects happen.

Drive failure is commonly linked to incorrect bit selection, worn tooling, excessive torque, poor alignment, coating buildup, or uncontrolled recess geometry.

Common Drive Issues

Cam-Out

Tool slips out of the recess, reducing torque control and damaging the head.

Drive Rounding

Drive corners deform due to excessive torque, repeated use, or poor bit fit.

Recess Filling

Coating, plating, paint, or contamination reduces proper tool engagement.

Head Damage

Over-tightening, misalignment, or tooling error can make removal difficult.

Head Markings

Identification, strength recognition, and traceability built into the fastener.

Head markings use symbols, letters, numbers, radial lines, or patterns to communicate strength, material, manufacturer identity, standard reference, and inspection confidence.

Purpose of Head Markings

Markings help users identify the fastener before installation, during inspection, and throughout service life. Correct marking reduces mix-ups and supports quality assurance.

  • Manufacturer identification
  • Property class or grade
  • Material category
  • Stainless steel group
  • Structural classification
  • Traceability requirement

Manufacturer Identification

Manufacturer marks help trace the fastener back to its producer and support accountability, supply-chain verification, and regulated market confidence.

Marking Methods

Clear markings without compromising head integrity.

Marking method depends on fastener size, head style, material, standard requirement, production process, and readability after coating or surface treatment.

01

Raised Markings

Formed during heading or forging. Durable and commonly used on bolts and screws.

02

Depressed Markings

Stamped or indented into the fastener surface where raised marks are not suitable.

03

Laser Marking

Used for precision identification, traceability, branding, and special application needs.

04

Stamping

Used for grade, manufacturer, batch, or specification identification depending on fastener type.
