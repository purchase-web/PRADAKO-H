01 / Fundamental Difference

Temperature changes the metal’s resistance to deformation.

Cold forging retains strong work hardening. Warm forging deliberately lowers flow stress while preserving good control. Hot forging works above the recrystallisation regime to permit the most severe deformation.
Cold forging / near room temperature
LOW T

Precision window

Highest forming resistance, excellent dimensional control, minimal thermal scale and very high production potential.

Warm forging / intermediate temperature
MID T

Balance window

Lower flow stress than cold forming with better surface condition and dimensional control than conventional hot forging.

Hot forging / above recrystallisation
HIGH T

Formability window

Maximum metal flow, lower deformation resistance and the widest practical capability for large or severe geometries.

Cold forging

Precision + productivity

Best suited where stable high-volume production, tight dimensions, excellent surface condition and material utilisation dominate the decision.

  • Highest forming loads
  • Strong work hardening
  • Excellent repeatability
  • Very low scale from forming
  • Highly suitable for automated multi-station headers
Typical fit Screws, bolts, pins, rivets, precision specials
Warm forging

Formability + control

Useful when cold-forming load, cracking or station count becomes excessive, but the precision and surface penalties of full hot forging are undesirable.

  • Reduced flow stress
  • Improved ductility
  • Lower punch and die loading
  • Less scale than conventional hot forging
  • Strong near-net-shape potential
Typical fit Severe heads, large flanges, difficult alloys, larger near-net parts
Hot forging

Maximum forming capability

The route to large cross-sections, demanding deformation, heavy structural geometry and materials that are difficult to shape economically at lower temperatures.

  • Lowest material flow stress
  • Highest deformation capability
  • Strong large-section feasibility
  • Greater thermal scale and contraction
  • More allowance for finishing may be required
Typical fit Large bolts, eye bolts, structural specials, heavy engineering forgings

Cold is not weak.
Hot is not automatically strong.

03 / Terminology

Forging is the family. Heading and extrusion are forming actions.

A modern fastener former is doing far more than simply “making a head.” The process may combine upsetting, extrusion, piercing, sizing and trimming across several stations.

A / FORGING
Forging

Broad family of manufacturing processes that plastically deform solid metal under compressive force.

B / HEADING
Heading

Upsets material axially so cross-sectional area grows — the classic route from wire or blank into a fastener head.

C / EXTRUSION
Extrusion

Controls the direction in which material flows through or around tooling: forward, backward or combined.

D / COLD FORMING
Cold forming

The broader production route combining cut-off, heading, extrusion, piercing, coining, trimming and sizing.

05 / Deformation Engineering

Temperature alone does not define the process response.

Metal behaviour during forging depends on strain, strain rate, temperature, friction, material condition and tool geometry acting together.

Flow stress

The resistance of the metal changes as deformation progresses. A useful conceptual relationship is:

σf = f( ε , ε̇ , T )
σf = flow stress ε = strain ε̇ = strain rate T = temperature

Deformation severity

Cold-formability is not determined by diameter alone. The real design variables include head volume, upset ratio, extrusion reduction, strain localisation and metal-flow path.

06 / Tribology

Forging happens at the interface between workpiece, lubricant and die.

Lubrication affects forming load, die fill, galling, heat generation, surface quality, metal flow and the life of punches and dies.

Why friction matters

Too much friction increases load and restricts material flow. Too little or poorly controlled lubrication can change die filling and repeatability. The objective is not simply “less friction,” but controlled friction.

LoadDie fillGallingHeatSurface finish

Temperature changes lubrication demands

Cold: strong surface-preparation and lubricant systems. Warm: lubricants must remain effective at intermediate temperature. Hot: the system must withstand extreme heat, scale interaction and thermal cycling.

07 / Multi-Station Forming

Do not force all the deformation into one violent hit.

Progressive forming distributes deformation, controls metal flow and can improve concentricity, tooling reliability and part complexity.

STATION 01Preform

Prepare the material path.

STATION 02Upset

Build head volume progressively.

STATION 03Intermediate

Control flow into key features.

STATION 04Final form

Achieve near-final head geometry.

STATION 05Trim / pierce

Complete geometry where needed.

08 / After Forging

Forging, threading and heat treatment are separate engineering decisions.

A hot-forged bolt may still have rolled threads. A cold-headed component may still require machining. Final strength comes from the complete route.

Rolled vs cut threads

Rolled threads plastically form the thread profile with no material removal and can deliver high productivity, good surface condition and favourable thread-root material flow when correctly controlled.

Cut threads remove material and remain useful for unusual geometry, very large sizes, lower volume or manufacturing routes centred on machining.

Forging does not set property class alone

For high-strength fasteners, final performance may depend on subsequent austenitising, quenching, tempering, case hardening, solution treatment or age hardening — depending on material and product requirements.

Forging ≠ final property class

09 / Structural Performance

The component must survive service — not merely leave the die correctly shaped.

Fatigue-critical regions, grain flow, thread roots, surface defects, decarburisation and residual stresses can matter as much as headline tensile strength.

Fatigue-sensitive locations

First engaged thread Thread roots Under-head fillet Head / shank transition Tool marks Corrosion pits

A route should be judged not only by whether it can create the geometry, but by the condition it leaves at the most highly stressed surfaces.

Grain flow

Forging can produce favourable material flow around the head, fillet and shank. But “forged grain flow” is not automatically good grain flow.

11 / Surface Integrity

Correct dimensions and hardness do not make a defective surface acceptable.

Cracks, seams, laps, folds, tool damage, scale pits, carburisation/decarburisation and burrs must be understood in context of the applicable product requirements.

Decarburisation

Elevated-temperature exposure can reduce surface carbon depending on atmosphere and thermal cycle. That may alter surface hardness, microstructure and fatigue-critical behaviour.

Surface carbon ↓ → surface response changes

Hydrogen embrittlement

Hydrogen risk is not simply a “cold-forging problem.” For susceptible high-strength steels, later processes such as acid cleaning, pickling and electroplating can become critical and must be controlled as part of the full manufacturing route.

17 / Myths vs Reality

Forging myths survive because they sound simple.

Manufacturing engineering is rarely that simple. Open each myth for the more accurate interpretation.

“Hot-forged fasteners are always stronger.”

False. Final strength depends on material, heat treatment, geometry, defects, microstructure and validation. Forging temperature alone does not determine strength.

“Cold forging is only for soft, low-strength bolts.”

False. Suitable alloy and stainless steels are intentionally supplied for cold heading, including materials intended for subsequent strengthening heat treatments.

“Cold-forged components are automatically brittle.”

Cold work reduces remaining ductility, but brittleness is not an unavoidable result. Material condition, deformation sequence and later treatment matter.

“Cold forging uses no heat.”

External billet heating may be absent, but plastic work and friction generate heat. Material preparation can also include thermal treatments.

“Warm forging is just poorly controlled hot forging.”

No. Warm forging is a deliberate operating window chosen to lower load while retaining better control and surface condition than conventional hot forging.

“The hottest billet is always easiest and best.”

Higher temperature can lower forming resistance, but excessive heat can create scale, decarburisation, grain growth, poor dimensional control or more serious metallurgical damage.

“Hot forging removes every internal defect.”

Compressive deformation can close some voids under suitable conditions, but inclusions, segregation, contaminated boundaries and all cracks are not magically removed.

“Forged means defect-free.”

Forgings can contain laps, folds, bursts, underfill, tool marks and other discontinuities. Forging improves structural potential; it does not eliminate process control.

“Cold forging always gives the longest die life.”

Cold dies avoid severe thermal cycling but see very high mechanical pressure. Hot dies see lower workpiece flow stress but major thermal fatigue, scale abrasion and heat checking.

“Hot forging is always inaccurate.”

Hot forging usually has wider thermal variation than cold forming, but precision hot-forging and calibration routes can produce very capable geometry.

“Large bolts must always be hot forged.”

No universal diameter decides the process. Geometry, material, deformation severity, machinery, tooling and volume determine feasibility.

“Stainless steel cannot be cold forged.”

Incorrect. Suitable stainless grades and delivery conditions are used specifically for cold heading and cold extrusion.

“More forming stations mean worse manufacturing.”

Often the opposite. Progressive deformation can reduce local severity, control material flow and improve tooling reliability.

“A harder bolt is always a better bolt.”

No. Strength, hardness, toughness, fatigue performance and susceptibility to certain failure mechanisms must be balanced for the application.

“A property class tells you the forging method.”

It does not. Property class communicates required mechanical performance; it is not a universal code for cold, warm or hot forging.

“One forging temperature range suits every steel.”

No. Alloy chemistry, microstructure, strain rate, deformation history and process timing affect the usable working window.

Final Engineering Conclusion

The best forging process is the one that makes the right part — repeatedly.

Cold forging is the precision and productivity route. Warm forging is the process-balancing route. Hot forging is the maximum-formability route. None is universally superior.
