01 — First Principles

What actually makes
steel stainless

Stainless steel is not corrosion-proof. It is corrosion-repairing. Above roughly 10.5% chromium, the alloy stops forming red iron oxide and starts forming a dense, transparent, tightly bonded chromium-oxide layer instead. That layer is the entire mechanism.

It forms itself

Chromium has a far higher affinity for oxygen than iron does. On exposure to air, chromium at the surface oxidises preferentially, producing a continuous Cr₂O₃ film a few nanometres thick — invisible, non-porous and firmly keyed to the metal beneath.

It survives damage

Machining, thread rolling, a dropped spanner — bare metal is exposed. On carbon steel that is where rust begins. On stainless, the exposed chromium simply repeats the reaction, provided oxygen can reach it.

It heals — if it can breathe

Re-passivation takes seconds in open air. Starve the surface of oxygen — a tight crevice, a paint film, a bed of dirt, standing water in a blind hole — and the film cannot regenerate. That is when stainless corrodes, and it is almost always a design failure rather than a material failure.

Design rule. Avoid slots, gaps, unventilated pockets, humidity traps and dirt deposits. Anything that blocks atmospheric oxygen from reaching the surface will degrade the corrosion performance of an otherwise correct grade.
Coatings cut both ways. Black oxidising, paint and heavy organic coatings seal oxygen out. Surface roughening has the same effect. Under chloride-bearing media the consequence can be crevice attack or stress corrosion cracking — often invisible until sudden failure.
02 — Metallurgical Families

Four structures,
four sets of rules

Crystal structure — not chemistry alone — decides whether a stainless fastener can be hardened, whether it is magnetic, and how it behaves in chloride. ISO 3506 organises every grade under one of four groups: austenitic (A), martensitic (C), ferritic (F) and duplex (D).

Austenitic

A1 · A2 · A3 · A4 · A5 · A8

The 300-series family, and around 97% of all stainless fasteners produced. High chromium with substantial nickel stabilises the face-centred cubic structure down to room temperature: excellent corrosion resistance, outstanding ductility and toughness, effectively non-magnetic. It cannot be hardened by heat treatment — strength comes only from cold work, which is why property classes are tied to cold forming rather than quench and temper.

Martensitic

C1 · C3 · C4

High chromium, very little nickel, and enough carbon to transform on quenching. These grades harden and temper to genuinely high strength — class 110 reaches 1100 MPa — at the cost of ductility and corrosion resistance. Strongly magnetic. Used where hardness, wear or cutting edges matter more than chloride resistance: pumps, turbines, valves, self-drilling points, retaining rings.

A sound economic compromise wherever A2-level performance is more than the application needs, and useful in some higher-chloride environments where austenitic grades are vulnerable to stress corrosion cracking.

Duplex

D2 · D4 · D6 · D8

Added to ISO 3506 in the 2020 revision. A roughly 40–60% mixed austenitic-ferritic microstructure with more chromium and less nickel than the A grades, alloyed with nitrogen. The result is roughly double the yield strength of A2 or A4, markedly better resistance to chloride stress corrosion cracking, and — for D6 and D8 — pitting resistance beyond A4. On magnetism. A2 and A4 are non-magnetic in the annealed wire, but cold heading and thread rolling deform the austenite and can induce a weak magnetic response — strongest at the head and in the rolled threads. It signals work hardening, not a change of grade, and it does not reduce corrosion resistance. Higher nickel suppresses it. Rejecting stainless fasteners because a magnet sticks is one of the most common inspection errors in the industry.
03 — Designation System

Every stainless
marking, decoded

Under ISO 3506 a designation is two blocks separated by a hyphen. The first block is the steel grade — a letter for the metallurgical group and a digit for the composition range within it. The second block is the property class: one tenth of the minimum tensile strength in MPa, or for nuts, one tenth of the stress under proof load.

CHROMIUM

The element that creates the passive film. Above 10.5% it dominates the surface chemistry; more chromium raises pitting resistance and, above about 17%, is the basis of every serious marine grade.

NICKEL

Stabilises the austenitic structure, delivering ductility, low-temperature toughness and non-magnetic behaviour. It also reduces work hardening, which is precisely why austenitic wire cold forms so well.

MOLYBDENUM

The A2-to-A4 difference. Two to three percent transforms resistance to chlorides and sulphates — seawater, de-icing salt, pulp and paper acids — and improves strength retention at temperature.

CARBON

Adds strength and costs corrosion resistance. Each unit of carbon ties up roughly seventeen times its weight in chromium as carbide, removing it from passive-film duty. Hence L grades and Ti/Nb stabilisation.

NITROGEN

The duplex enabler. A potent austenite stabiliser and solid-solution strengthener that lifts yield strength and pitting resistance without the cost of nickel.

SULPHUR

Deliberately raised in A1 and C4 to break chips and give free-machining behaviour. The manganese sulphide inclusions that make it machinable are also pit initiation sites — A1 is measurably less corrosion resistant than A2.

06 — Identification Markings

Read the head,
know the steel

Marking is mandatory — not decorative. Hexagon head bolts and screws, and hexagon or hexalobular socket head cap screws of nominal thread diameter 5 mm and above, must carry the steel grade and property class together with the manufacturer's identification mark. Studs from 6 mm and nuts from 5 mm follow the same rule.

Pradako fasteners carry the mark PM. Where you see PM in the illustrations below, that is our registered manufacturer's identification mark, applied during forging alongside the ISO 3506 grade and class. An unmarked stainless fastener carries no guarantee of class — in practice, unmarked product frequently performs only to class 50.
WHERE IT GOES

On hexagon heads, the mark is applied to the top face. On socket head cap screws, either the top face or — for small sizes — the alternative arrangement shown. On studs, the plain shank; where the shank cannot be marked, the grade alone may be applied to the nut end.

NUTS

Marking on one face only is acceptable and, on the bearing surface, must be applied by indentation. Marking on the side face is permitted as an alternative. Where A2 and A4 nuts carry groove marking without a class, class 50 or 025 applies by default.

LEFT-HAND THREAD

Left-hand threads are identified in accordance with ISO 898-1 for bolts, screws and studs and ISO 898-2 for nuts, in addition to the grade and class marking.

PACKAGING

Packaging is marked with the manufacturer's or distributor's identification, the grade and class symbol and the manufacturing lot number — the audit trail that ties a fastener in the field back to a melt and a forging batch.

08 — Failure Modes

How stainless
actually fails

General uniform rusting is almost never the problem. Stainless fails locally, at a scale of tenths of a millimetre, in ways that are difficult to see and rarely obvious until the joint lets go. Six mechanisms account for the overwhelming majority of field failures.

01 — PITTING

Chloride breaks the film locally

Chloride ions penetrate the passive layer at weak points — an inclusion, a scratch, a rolled-in surface defect. The pit becomes self-sustaining: acidic, oxygen-starved and deepening. Molybdenum is the principal defence, which is why A4 exists.

  • Raise Mo — A2 → A4 → D6 → A8
  • Eliminate embedded iron and scale
  • Keep surfaces clean and freely rinsed
02 — CREVICE

Geometry starves the film of oxygen

Under a washer, in a thread engagement, beneath a gasket or a deposit of dirt. Oxygen cannot reach the surface, the film cannot regenerate, and attack proceeds inside the gap. Crevice corrosion begins at chloride levels and temperatures far below those needed to pit an open surface.

  • Design out gaps and unventilated pockets
  • Seal or fully weld overlapping joints
  • Prefer a higher-alloy grade over a tighter crevice
03 — STRESS CORROSION CRACKING

The one that fails without warning

Tensile stress plus chloride plus temperature — typically above roughly 50–60 °C — produces branched cracking through the section with almost no visible surface evidence and no ductility at fracture. A preloaded bolt supplies the stress by definition. Standard A2 and A4 are the vulnerable grades; duplex and A8 are markedly better.

  • Indoor pool atmospheres are the classic case
  • Specify A8 or duplex for chloride plus heat plus preload
  • Avoid martensitic grades in this service entirely
04 — INTERGRANULAR

Sensitisation at the grain boundary

Held roughly between 425 °C and 815 °C, carbon in a non-stabilised grade combines with chromium to form carbide at grain boundaries, locally starving the adjacent metal of the chromium it needs to passivate. Welding is the usual culprit; a hot service environment does it slowly.

  • Specify A3 or A5 (Ti/Nb stabilised)
  • Or an L grade with C ≤ 0.03%
  • Testing to ISO 3651-1 / -2 where risk exists
05 — GALVANIC

The wrong couple in the wrong electrolyte

Stainless is noble. Couple it to aluminium, zinc or plain steel with an electrolyte present and the less noble member corrodes — accelerated sharply when a small anode area meets a large cathode. A stainless bolt in an aluminium plate is the textbook example and is usually acceptable; a stainless plate on aluminium fasteners is not.

  • Keep the fastener the noble, small-area member
  • Isolate with sleeves, washers or coatings
  • Check the pairing in our galvanic matrix
06 — HYDROGEN EMBRITTLEMENT

A martensitic problem, not an austenitic one

Hardened martensitic grades absorb atomic hydrogen during pickling, plating or in-service cathodic protection, and crack under sustained load — often hours or days after assembly. Austenitic grades are essentially immune at ambient temperature. Duplex sits between the two and needs care under cathodic protection subsea.

  • Control pickling and plating processes
  • Bake after plating where specified
  • Prefer mechanical cleaning on hardened parts
Free iron is the most common avoidable cause of rust on stainless. Carbon steel particles picked up from tooling, grinding dust, transport or storage embed in the surface and rust in place — the part appears to be corroding when it is in fact contaminated. Passivation to ISO 16048 dissolves the embedded iron and restores a fully chromium-rich surface. Stainless should never share tooling, blast media or storage racks with carbon steel.
09 — Assembly Behaviour

Stainless does not
tighten like steel

The single most expensive mistake with stainless fasteners is applying carbon-steel torque practice to them. Friction is different, ductility is different, and a seized assembly usually has to be cut out.

GALLING & COLD WELDING

Austenitic stainless has a tough, self-regenerating oxide and high ductility. Under thread contact pressure the film is wiped away and clean metal meets clean metal, which cold welds on the spot. The nut locks halfway down and neither tightens nor releases. It happens most on coarse threads, in larger diameters, at speed, and when both parts are the same grade at the same hardness.

  • Lubricate — nickel or moly anti-seize, PTFE, wax
  • Pair unlike hardness: cold-worked bolt with softer nut
  • Run down slowly; avoid impact drivers
  • Rolled threads gall less than cut threads
  • Keep threads free of grit; chamfer and deburr
TORQUE, FRICTION & PRELOAD

Torque is only a proxy for preload, and on stainless a poor one: dry stainless-on-stainless friction is both high and highly scattered, so a large share of applied torque never becomes clamp load. Lubrication lowers the torque needed for a given preload dramatically — which means a lubricated joint tightened to an unlubricated torque figure will be over-stressed and may yield or gall.

  • Always state the friction condition with the torque
  • Derive torque from target preload, not from a table
  • Class 70 A2/A4 yields well below an 8.8 bolt
  • Consider angle-controlled or preload-controlled tightening
MIXING GRADES

Bolt and nut should be matched in class — a class 70 bolt with a class 50 nut is limited by the nut, and the joint will strip before the bolt reaches its capability. Mixing grades across a joint also opens galvanic questions: A2 and A4 together in a wet chloride environment will preferentially attack the A2.

  • Nut class ≥ bolt class
  • Thin nuts carry reduced loadability by definition
  • Washers should match the fastener grade

10 — Surface & Finish

Finishing a metal
that finishes itself

Stainless does not need a sacrificial coating, and adding one usually costs more corrosion performance than it buys. The finishes that matter are those that clean, restore or thicken the passive layer.

PASSIVATION · ISO 16048

Immersion in an oxidising acid — traditionally nitric, increasingly citric — that dissolves free iron and sulphide inclusions from the surface and allows a uniform, chromium-rich oxide to re-form. It removes manufacturing contamination rather than adding a layer. Passivated fasteners may carry a P suffix.

ELECTROPOLISHING

Anodic dissolution that levels the surface at a microscopic scale, removing peaks, embedded particles and residual smearing. It raises the chromium-to-iron ratio at the surface, improves cleanability and measurably increases pitting resistance — the standard for pharmaceutical, semiconductor and high-purity process hardware.

BLACK PASSIVATION

A conversion finish giving a black or dark transparent appearance with improved performance in acidic environments. Common in battery hardware and similar equipment where a black aesthetic and acid exposure coincide.

MECHANICAL FINISHES

Bright drawn, tumbled, vibratory polished or blasted with stainless or ceramic media. Media selection is critical: carbon-steel shot embeds iron and guarantees future rust staining. Rough finishes trap contaminants and reduce corrosion performance.

PTFE & ANTI-GALLING

Dry-film PTFE, wax and moly coatings applied for a controlled, repeatable friction coefficient and to prevent thread seizure. A functional coating rather than a corrosion coating — but by preventing galling it protects the joint far more effectively than a plating would.

WHAT TO AVOID

Zinc plating, heavy paint and black oxidising all seal oxygen away from the surface and defeat the mechanism that makes the material stainless. Where an appearance requirement forces a coating, expect a corrosion penalty and specify accordingly.

11 — Reference

Stainless steel
terminology

The working vocabulary of stainless fastener engineering, as used on drawings, in specifications and on inspection reports.

Austenitic

The 300-series family. A face-centred cubic structure stabilised by nickel, formed at high temperature and retained at room temperature. Cannot be hardened by heat treatment; effectively non-magnetic; the highest general corrosion resistance in the standard fastener range. Named after the metallurgist Sir William Chandler Roberts-Austen.

Carbide precipitation

Formation of chromium carbide at grain boundaries when the steel is held roughly between 425 °C and 815 °C. It removes chromium from the adjacent metal and leaves those boundaries unable to passivate. See sensitisation.

Cold forming / cold heading

Forming a fastener below the recrystallisation temperature by driving wire against dies at high speed. It raises tensile strength and hardness through work hardening and reduces ductility — the mechanism behind property classes 70, 80 and 100 in austenitic and duplex grades.

Creep

Slow, continuing deformation under sustained load at elevated temperature. In a bolted joint it appears as preload relaxation over time without any rotation of the nut.

Crevice corrosion

Localised attack inside a narrow gap where the electrolyte becomes stagnant and oxygen-depleted, preventing re-passivation. Initiates at lower chloride concentrations and temperatures than pitting on an open surface.

Discontinuity

Surface imperfection such as a pit, tool mark, void, lap, fold, seam or inclusion. Minor discontinuities within the limits of ISO 6157 are permissible on commercial fasteners; those exceeding them are defects.

Drawing

Pulling wire rod through a die to reduce it to the precise diameter required for the fastener to be formed. It also imparts a controlled amount of work hardening before heading.

Ductility

The ability to deform plastically before fracture. In fastener terms it is the reserve that allows a joint to redistribute load rather than fail abruptly, and it is measured as elongation after fracture.

Duplex

A roughly balanced two-phase austenitic-ferritic microstructure, nitrogen alloyed. Approximately double the yield strength of standard austenitic grades with substantially better resistance to chloride stress corrosion cracking. Grades D2, D4, D6 and D8.

Elongation after fracture (A)

Permanent extension at the point of rupture, expressed for stainless fasteners as a multiple of nominal thread diameter — 0.6 d, 0.4 d, 0.3 d or 0.2 d depending on class. Measured on the whole fastener, not a machined test piece. Elongation falls as strength and hardness rise.

Ferritic

A body-centred cubic chromium steel with little or no nickel and low carbon. Not hardenable by heat treatment, magnetic, moderate corrosion resistance, and economical because it carries no nickel cost. Grade F1.

Free machining

Deliberate sulphur addition — 0.15% to 0.35% in A1 and C4 — that breaks chips and extends tool life. The manganese sulphide inclusions responsible also act as corrosion initiation sites, so free-machining grades are always less corrosion resistant than their equivalents.

Galling

Adhesive wear in the thread: the passive film is wiped away under contact pressure, clean metal meets clean metal and cold welds. The assembly seizes mid-run and can rarely be recovered. Austenitic grades are particularly prone.

Magnetism

Austenitic grades are non-magnetic as annealed wire. Cold heading and thread rolling can induce a weak magnetic response, concentrated in the head and rolled threads. It indicates work hardening and does not reduce corrosion resistance or indicate a wrong grade.

Martensitic

A hardenable body-centred tetragonal structure produced by quenching a high-chromium, higher-carbon stainless. Strong and magnetic, with limited corrosion resistance and sensitivity to hydrogen embrittlement. Grades C1, C3, C4.

Passivation

Controlled chemical treatment to ISO 16048 that removes free iron and other surface contamination and allows a uniform chromium oxide film to form. It cleans rather than coats.

Passive film

The chromium-rich oxide layer, on the order of two nanometres thick, that forms spontaneously in the presence of oxygen and re-forms when damaged. It is the entire basis of stainless corrosion resistance.

Pitting

Localised breakdown of the passive film, typically by chloride, producing narrow deep cavities. Very little mass is lost, yet a pit can penetrate a section and act as a fatigue crack initiator.

PREN

Pitting Resistance Equivalent Number, commonly Cr + 3.3 Mo + 16 N. A comparative index for ranking grades by pitting resistance. ISO 3506 uses the same relation to separate lean duplex D2 from D4 — for classification only, not as a selection criterion.

Proof load

A specified test load that a fastener must sustain without measurable permanent set. For nuts it is expressed as stress under proof load, Sp, and is the basis of the nut property class.

Property class

The second block of an ISO 3506 designation. For bolts, screws and studs it is one tenth of the minimum tensile strength in MPa; for nuts, one tenth of the stress under proof load. A leading zero indicates reduced loadability.

Sensitisation

The condition produced by carbide precipitation, in which grain boundaries are locally depleted of chromium and become susceptible to intergranular corrosion. Prevented by stabilisation with Ti/Nb (A3, A5) or by low carbon (L grades).

Stainless steel

Steel containing at least 10.5% chromium and not more than 1.2% carbon, in which chromium forms a passive surface oxide instead of red iron oxide. Corrosion resistance is a surface phenomenon, not a bulk property.

Stabilised grade

A grade containing titanium, niobium or tantalum in sufficient quantity to combine with carbon before chromium can — preserving chromium for passivation after welding or hot exposure. Grades A3 and A5.

Stress area

The nominal cross-section on which thread strength is calculated, derived from the mean of the pitch and minor diameters. All ISO 3506 tensile values are referenced to it.

Stress corrosion cracking

Cracking under the combined action of tensile stress, a specific corrosive medium — chloride for austenitic stainless — and, usually, elevated temperature. Failure is brittle and gives almost no external warning.

Tensile strength (Rm)

The maximum stress the fastener sustains before fracture, calculated on the nominal stress area of the thread. The figure the property class is derived from.

Torsional strength

The torque required to twist a fastener to failure. Minimum breaking torque values are tabulated for austenitic grades M1.6 to M16. It is a material property, never a tightening instruction.

Work hardening

The increase in strength and hardness, and loss of ductility, produced by plastic deformation. Austenitic stainless work hardens rapidly, which is both how property class 70 is achieved and why it galls and machines poorly.

Yield strength (Rp0.2)

Formally, the stress producing 0.2% non-proportional permanent strain. For a bolted joint it is the practical ceiling on preload — go beyond it and clamp load becomes unpredictable.
