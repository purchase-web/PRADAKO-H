Heat Treatment Processes

Four process routes, four performance goals

Each heat-treatment process has a specific purpose. The correct route depends on whether the fastener needs surface durability, full-section strength, forming stability, or final toughness.

01
Surface Hardness

Case Hardening

Creates a hard, wear-resistant outer surface while maintaining a tougher and more ductile core. Ideal for fasteners that need strong thread engagement and surface durability.

Hard outside Tougher core Better wear resistance

Common Uses

  • Self-tapping screws
  • Self-drilling screws
  • Thread-forming screws
  • Sheet metal screws

Benefits

  • Higher surface wear resistance
  • Better thread engagement
  • Improved recess durability
  • Surface deformation control

Quality Checks

  • Surface hardness
  • Core hardness
  • Case depth
  • Torsion performance
02
Full Section Strength

Through Hardening

Develops strength across the complete cross-section of the fastener. Used where the whole fastener body must carry load reliably.

Uniform strength Proof load capacity Structural reliability

Common Uses

  • Property Class 8.8
  • Property Class 10.9
  • Property Class 12.9
  • Socket and structural bolts

Benefits

  • Uniform cross-section strength
  • Higher load capacity
  • Improved fatigue reliability
  • Stronger core performance

Quality Checks

  • Core hardness
  • Tensile strength
  • Proof load
  • Decarburization inspection
03
Forming Stability

Annealing

Softens material, improves ductility, and reduces internal stresses before forming, drawing, heading, tapping, machining, or further heat treatment.

Better ductility Reduced cracking Improved forming

Common Uses

  • Wire drawing preparation
  • Cold heading wire
  • Cold forged bolts and screws
  • Nuts before forming or tapping

Benefits

  • Improved ductility
  • Better cold-forming behavior
  • Lower crack risk
  • Improved machinability

Quality Checks

  • Wire hardness
  • Microstructure
  • Surface condition
  • Formability verification
04
Hardness Balance

Tempering

Performed after hardening or quenching to reduce brittleness, relieve internal stresses, improve toughness, and adjust hardness to the final required range.

Reduced brittleness Better toughness Final hardness control

Common Uses

  • High tensile bolts
  • Socket screws
  • Structural fasteners
  • Studs and threaded rods

Benefits

  • Improves toughness
  • Controls final hardness
  • Improves fatigue resistance
  • Supports property class performance

Quality Checks

  • Final hardness
  • Tensile strength
  • Proof load
  • Retempering verification

Technical Notes

Important engineering considerations

These details stay hidden until the user wants deeper technical reading, keeping the main page clean.

What happens if heat treatment is not controlled properly?
Improper heat treatment can cause brittle failure, poor proof load performance, thread damage, surface cracking, dimensional distortion, low toughness, excessive hardness, insufficient strength, or premature fatigue failure.
Why is tempering important after hardening?
After quenching, steel can become very hard but brittle. Tempering reduces brittleness, relieves internal stresses, improves toughness, and adjusts hardness to the required final range.
Why is annealing used before cold forming?
Annealing improves ductility and reduces hardness before forming. This helps wire and bar material flow more predictably during drawing, heading, tapping, machining, and thread production.
How is case hardening different from through hardening?
Case hardening primarily strengthens the surface while keeping the core tougher. Through hardening develops strength across the full fastener section for higher load-bearing applications.
