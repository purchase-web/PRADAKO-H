Chapter 01

Fundamentals & Tribology

Fastener friction is not a decorative surface characteristic. It is a functional engineering property that governs how much of the applied tightening torque becomes useful clamp load.

What is the coefficient of friction?

The coefficient of friction, represented by μ, is a dimensionless value describing resistance to sliding between contacting surfaces. In a bolted joint, friction is normally considered at two principal interfaces: the mating thread flanks and the rotating bearing surface beneath the bolt head, screw head, nut or washer.

μth — thread friction

Sliding resistance between external and internal thread flanks.

μb — bearing friction

Sliding resistance beneath the rotating head, nut or washer.

μtot — total friction

An equivalent value representing the complete defined test arrangement.

Fastener friction is a tribology problem

Tribology deals with friction, lubrication, wear and the interaction of surfaces in relative motion. A machined or coated surface that appears smooth is actually made of microscopic high points called asperities. During tightening, those asperities contact, deform, shear, polish, tear or weld locally as the thread flanks and bearing surfaces slide.

  1. Asperities first contact one another.
  2. Local contact pressure becomes extremely high.
  3. Oxides, topcoats and lubricant films deform or break.
  4. Microscopic junctions can form between surfaces.
  5. Sliding redistributes lubricant and coating material.
  6. Heat and wear particles may be generated.
  7. The resulting friction changes the torque required to produce preload.

The measured friction value is therefore not a material constant of the steel alone. It belongs to the complete surface and assembly system.

Static, kinetic and apparent engineering friction

Static friction

Static friction is the resistance that must be overcome before relative movement begins. It influences breakaway torque, restart torque after a pause, removal torque and some forms of stick-slip behaviour.

Kinetic or sliding friction

Once motion begins, kinetic friction governs sliding. During continuous tightening, thread and bearing friction are predominantly sliding-friction phenomena.

Apparent engineering coefficient

Fastener torque–clamp-force testing generally produces an effective or apparent friction value calculated from measured torque, clamp force and defined geometry. It should not be treated as universal. The same coated bolt may produce different results when tested against coated steel, bare steel, aluminium, a hardened washer or a painted production surface.

The practical objective

Do not chase the lowest friction number. Engineer a stable friction window that repeatedly produces the required preload without exceeding the permissible load of the bolt, threads, washer or joint.

Chapter 02

Torque, Preload & Friction

Tightening torque is only an indirect method of controlling bolt tension. The applied torque is divided between thread geometry, thread friction and bearing friction.

Preload is the real objective

When a bolt or screw is tightened, it elongates and develops tensile load. The clamped parts are compressed by an approximately corresponding force. This initial force is called preload or clamp force.

A substantial proportion of tightening torque is consumed in overcoming friction. Only the remaining portion contributes to the mechanical work required to advance the thread and stretch the fastener.

MA = Mpitch + Mthread friction + Mbearing friction

Engineering torque–preload relationship

For a conventional metric fastener without a prevailing-torque feature, an engineering form of the relationship can be expressed as:

MA = FM [ P/(2π) + μthd2/(2 cos(α/2)) + μbDbm/2 ]
MA applied tightening torque FM assembly preload P thread pitch d2 thread pitch diameter α included thread angle Dbm effective bearing friction diameter μth thread coefficient of friction μb bearing coefficient of friction

For a standard ISO metric thread, the included thread angle is 60°. This relationship demonstrates why identical nominal friction cannot automatically produce identical tightening behaviour across different pitches, head diameters, flange diameters, washers or bearing geometries.

Coefficient of friction versus nut factor

A simplified tightening relationship frequently used for quick estimates is:

MA = K FM d

Here K is the nut factor or torque coefficient. It is not the same as coefficient of friction μ.

ParameterMeaningWhat it contains
μCoefficient of frictionSliding behaviour at a defined interface under defined conditions.
KNut factor / torque coefficientCombined empirical influence of pitch, thread geometry, friction, bearing diameter and test configuration.
K ≠ μ

A nut factor established for one bolt–nut–washer system should not be transferred to another geometry without validation.

What happens when friction changes at the same tightening torque?

Controlled friction

Torque is converted into preload within a validated, repeatable range. The design objective is predictable clamp load with low scatter.

Preload window and friction scatter

Torque-controlled tightening should be considered statistically. The design must accommodate the minimum and maximum expected assembly preload:

FM,min > Frequired   and   FM,max < Fpermissible

The lower boundary must still prevent separation, slip or leakage. The upper boundary must remain below bolt yielding, thread stripping, crushing, excessive gasket compression or other permissible limits. A wide friction range can make it impossible to satisfy both conditions with one torque value.

Chapter 03

Thread & Bearing Interfaces

Thread friction and bearing friction have different geometries, different moment arms and different sensitivities. Both must be controlled.

Thread friction in depth

Thread pitch

Coarse and fine threads have different helix geometry, pitch torque and sensitivity to dimensional variation.

Pitch diameter

Changes the moment arm at which thread friction acts.

Thread flank angle

Creates a wedging effect and affects the normal force at the engaged flanks.

Thread tolerance

Influences clearance, coating allowance, interference risk and contact pattern.

Thread roughness

Can increase mechanical interlocking, coating wear, friction scatter and galling tendency.

Thread damage

Burrs, dents, laps or incomplete threads can create torque without corresponding preload.

False torque

A tool can indicate the specified torque even when abnormal thread interference or damage has consumed that torque instead of creating useful clamp force.

Bearing-surface friction in depth

Bearing friction develops beneath rotating bolt heads, screw heads, nuts, washers, flanges and collars. It depends on both coefficient of friction and effective friction diameter.

  • A larger flange or washer increases the effective friction radius and can change the torque required for a given preload.
  • Soft materials such as aluminium, copper, polymers and coated sheet can deform beneath the bearing surface.
  • Paint, e-coat, powder coat or other films may crack, creep, plough or be displaced during tightening.
  • Washer rotation state matters: the washer may rotate with the nut, remain fixed, polish the substrate or deform.

For these reasons, a friction test against a polished laboratory washer may not represent an actual painted, plated or soft production joint.

Important friction-related parameters

ParameterMeaning
μthCoefficient of friction at the thread flanks.
μbCoefficient of friction beneath the rotating bearing surface.
μtotEquivalent total friction derived from the defined complete test arrangement.
KEmpirical torque coefficient or nut factor.
Prevailing torqueTorque required to rotate a locking feature before seating.
Breakaway torqueTorque required to initiate movement after rest, ageing or cure.
Removal torqueTorque measured during disassembly.

Two fastening systems can show a similar total coefficient but divide friction differently between the threads and bearing surface. That difference becomes important when head diameter, washer size, thread pitch or lubrication location changes.

Why the washer is an active part of the friction system

A washer can change effective bearing diameter, distribute pressure, prevent indentation, alter the sliding interface and modify friction scatter. It can rotate with the nut or remain stationary while the nut slides over it. Washer hardness, flatness, coating, roughness and geometry should therefore be part of the test specification.

Chapter 04

Coatings, Lubrication & Surface Engineering

Corrosion resistance and friction performance are related, but they are not the same requirement. A coating can pass a salt-spray test and still fail the assembly friction requirement.

Friction is a system property

System elementPossible influence
Bolt or screw coatingChanges thread and bearing lubricity, roughness and surface-film behaviour.
Nut / tapped-hole finishChanges mating-thread friction and adhesion tendency.
WasherChanges bearing friction, effective diameter and embedment.
Joint surfacePaint, aluminium, steel, polymer and composite surfaces behave differently.
LubricantCan sharply reduce friction and change preload at a fixed torque.
Topcoat / sealerCan provide deliberately controlled lubricity.
Thread-locking compoundMay lubricate while uncured and resist rotation after cure.
Tightening speedCan change film behaviour, local temperature and stick-slip.
Installation cyclesCan polish, remove, transfer or damage coating and lubricant.
Storage / temperatureCan alter waxes, oils, resins, oxidation and contamination.

Coating-system architecture

Friction is often controlled by the outermost working layers, not by the metallic coating alone. A typical engineered coating system may contain:

  1. Steel substrate
  2. Zinc or zinc-alloy deposit
  3. Conversion coating
  4. Sealer
  5. Topcoat
  6. Integral lubricant
  7. Subsequently applied lubricant

Each layer can influence corrosion resistance, friction, appearance, electrical behaviour, adhesion, chemical resistance, thread fit and torque–preload performance.

Major coating families and friction behaviour

Electroplated zinc

Friction can change significantly with conversion coating, sealer, wax, oil, topcoat, ageing and storage.

Zinc-nickel

High corrosion performance does not automatically define friction; the final sealer, topcoat and lubricant remain critical.

Zinc-iron

Surface hardness, conversion system and lubricant can produce behaviour different from conventional zinc.

Zinc-flake

Topcoats often provide controlled friction; the complete system must be validated for both corrosion and assembly behaviour.

Phosphate + oil

Oil retention can provide useful assembly performance, but coating weight, crystal structure, drainage and oil quantity require control.

Black oxide

Assembly friction often depends primarily on the supplementary oil or wax rather than the oxide layer alone.

Hot-dip galvanizing

Thick zinc, surface roughness, nut overtapping, thread allowance and lubrication strongly influence tightening.

Stainless steel

Galling sensitivity makes lubrication, material pairing and tightening speed particularly important.

Topcoats, waxes, oils, anti-seize and integral lubricants

A technically complete specification should distinguish among integral lubricant, lubricated topcoat, post-applied wax, oil, dry-film lubricant, anti-seize compound and customer-applied assembly lubricant.

The lubricant system should identify product designation, application method, target quantity, curing or drying condition, storage limitations, reapplication policy, temperature limits and compatibility with adhesives or sealants. The word “lubricated” by itself is not an adequate engineering requirement.

Storage, ageing and environmental effects

Friction can change after coating because of lubricant migration, wax hardening, solvent evaporation, surface oxidation, moisture absorption, packaging interaction, contamination, temperature cycling, ultraviolet exposure and handling damage.

Where the application is sensitive, testing may be required immediately after coating, after a defined conditioning period, after storage, after humidity exposure, after thermal exposure and at the actual point of assembly.

Tightening-speed and temperature effects

Tightening speed can affect interface temperature, lubricant-film behaviour, stick-slip, polymer topcoats, liquid adhesives and prevailing torque. A slow laboratory test may not represent a high-speed production spindle.

Temperature can change lubricant viscosity, resin hardness, wax state, surface-film strength and polymer locking features. Assembly-temperature behaviour and service-temperature behaviour should be considered separately.

Reuse and repeated tightening cycles

Repeated assembly can polish surfaces, remove lubricant, wear through topcoats, generate debris, reduce prevailing torque or increase galling tendency. If reuse is permitted, the specification should define cycle count, relubrication policy, friction or torque limits after each cycle, visual damage criteria and replacement requirements.

Chapter 05

Failure Mechanics & Joint Reliability

Both low and high friction can be dangerous. The failure mechanism depends on how friction changes the preload and the stresses introduced during tightening.

When friction is too low

  • Excessive preload at the specified torque
  • Bolt yielding or installation fracture
  • Thread stripping or nut dilation
  • Head or bearing-surface crushing
  • Gasket over-compression
  • Joint distortion
  • Tapped-hole pull-out
  • Coating extrusion or excessive embedment

When friction is too high

  • Insufficient preload
  • Joint opening or transverse slip
  • Self-loosening under vibration
  • Gasket leakage
  • Fretting and reduced fatigue life
  • Loose electrical / thermal joints
  • Noise, movement and misalignment
  • Uneven multi-bolt joint loading

Why friction scatter can be worse than an incorrect average

A batch with a wide spread can contain both low-friction fasteners at risk of overloading and high-friction fasteners at risk of under-tightening even when the average value looks acceptable.

The real production objective is therefore a controlled friction window with low scatter.

Stick-slip behaviour

Stick-slip occurs when contact alternates between sticking and sliding. Signs include jerky tightening, fluctuating torque, squeaking, sudden torque drops, irregular torque–angle curves and unstable preload.

Potential causes include inadequate lubrication, lubricant-film breakdown, rough surfaces, stainless-steel galling, damaged coatings, inappropriate tightening speed, intermittent tool operation and incompatible material combinations.

Galling and cold welding

Galling is severe adhesive wear in which material transfers between mating surfaces. The progression can include protective-film breakdown, local metal-to-metal adhesion, material transfer, surface tearing, rapid torque increase and eventual seizure or fracture.

Controls include suitable lubrication, appropriate material pairing, hardness difference, coating selection, controlled tightening speed, clean threads and limits on repeated reuse.

Combined tensile and torsional stress

During tightening, a bolt experiences both axial tensile stress and torsional shear stress. A useful equivalent-stress form is:

σeq = √(σt2 + 3τ2)

This explains why an assembly can yield during tightening even when a calculation based only on axial tensile stress appears acceptable.

Joint stiffness, preload retention and relaxation

Friction controls how much preload is generated initially, but joint stiffness determines how load is distributed after assembly. Important variables include bolt stiffness, clamped-part stiffness, external-load sharing, embedment, relaxation, creep, gasket compression and differential thermal expansion.

A perfectly controlled initial preload can still fall after assembly because of paint creep, soft washers, gasket relaxation, polymer deformation or microscopic surface flattening.

Friction, fatigue and self-loosening

Proper preload helps reduce the fluctuating stress experienced by a fastener. Insufficient preload can permit joint separation, transverse movement, bending and fretting, all of which can accelerate fatigue.

Rotational self-loosening should not be reduced to the statement “low friction causes loosening.” It is strongly influenced by transverse movement, preload, interface slip, vibration amplitude, joint geometry and locking features. Increasing friction indiscriminately may actually reduce preload and make loosening more likely.

Chapter 06

Special Fastener Systems

Conventional bolt–nut equations do not fully describe every fastener. Locking, forming, drilling and special bearing geometries add additional torque components.

Prevailing-torque fasteners

All-metal locknuts, polymer-insert nuts, deformed-thread nuts, locking patches and interference-thread systems require torque before the fastener seats.

Tinstallation = Tprevailing + Tthread + Tbearing + Tpreload

Prevailing torque does not itself create useful clamp load while the fastener is free-running. Assembly documents should state clearly whether the specified tightening value means total indicated torque or torque above prevailing torque.

Fastener-specific friction considerations

Fastener systemAdditional considerations
Flange bolts / nutsLarger effective bearing diameter can substantially change bearing torque.
SEMS screwsCaptive washer may rotate with the screw or slide independently against the joint.
Countersunk screwsConical bearing interface creates radial forces and different friction geometry.
Self-tapping screwsInstallation torque includes thread-forming or cutting torque before clamp force develops.
Thread-forming screwsMaterial displacement and prevailing interaction must be separated from seating and clamp-load behaviour.
Self-drilling screwsDrilling, thread-forming, seating and clamp-load torque occur sequentially.
Locking patchesPatch position, length, material and cure condition influence installation and removal torque.
Structural fastenersInstallation methods may use calibrated systems, direct tension indicators or other procedures rather than generic torque alone.
Aerospace fastenersCritical applications may require more direct control by elongation, tension or validated torque–angle methods.
Chapter 07

Tightening Methods & Stress Control

Torque-only tightening is common because it is practical, but it is also one of the methods most sensitive to friction variation.

Comparison of tightening methods

MethodMain control parameterFriction sensitivity
Torque controlFinal torqueHigh
Torque–angleTorque plus rotation after a defined pointModerate after snug point
Yield controlChange in tightening gradientLower, application-specific
Direct tensionMeasured bolt loadLow
Bolt elongationChange in bolt lengthLow
Hydraulic tensioningApplied tensile forceRelatively low
Tension-indicating washerWasher deformationSystem-dependent
Ultrasonic measurementBolt elongation / acoustic timeLow when correctly calibrated

Friction remains relevant even for more direct methods because it can influence installation torque, torsional stress, galling and surface damage. The key advantage is that the final preload is not inferred solely from torque.

Why generic torque tables can be misleading

Generic tables commonly assume a particular nut factor, lubrication state or surface condition. If the actual coating, topcoat, washer or lubricant differs, the resulting preload may differ substantially.

Critical-joint rule

For automotive, aerospace, defence, energy, pressure-containing and structural applications, establish tightening values from representative torque–clamp-force testing or a validated joint-calculation method rather than copying generic torque tables.

Chapter 08

ISO 16047 Testing & Curve Interpretation

A meaningful friction value requires a defined test system. Changing the mating thread, washer, bearing surface, speed or lubricant can change the result.

What torque–clamp-force testing can determine

Total tightening torque

Torque applied by the drive system.

Thread torque

Torque attributable to the threaded interface.

Bearing torque

Torque attributable to the rotating bearing interface.

Clamp force

Axial force generated in the test assembly.

Friction coefficients

Thread, bearing and total equivalent values where applicable.

Torque coefficient

Empirical torque–preload parameter for the defined system.

Test specification checklist

  1. Fastener size, property class and geometry
  2. Nut, insert or tapped-hole specification
  3. Washer material, hardness, finish and dimensions
  4. Bearing-surface material and condition
  5. Coating and lubricant designation
  6. Tightening speed
  7. Target clamp force or evaluation range
  8. Test temperature
  9. Number of samples
  10. First installation or repeated installation
  11. Cleaning / conditioning procedure
  12. Whether additional lubrication is permitted
  13. Acceptance limits for average and individual results
  14. Required reporting of scatter and outliers
Detailed torque–clamp-force test sequence
  1. Identify and trace the sample lot.
  2. Define the test bolt or screw.
  3. Define the mating nut or internal thread.
  4. Define the washer and bearing plate.
  5. Record all relevant surface finishes and coatings.
  6. Condition samples as specified.
  7. Set the defined tightening speed.
  8. Verify load and torque sensor calibration.
  9. Install with the specified lubrication state.
  10. Measure torque and clamp force continuously through tightening.
  11. Measure thread torque separately where the test system supports it.
  12. Determine bearing torque and friction parameters using the applicable equations.
  13. Repeat across the required sample count.
  14. Evaluate individual results, mean, scatter and abnormal curves.
  15. Report every relevant test condition so the result is reproducible.

Reading torque–angle and torque–clamp-force curves

Curve behaviourPossible interpretation
High running torqueDamaged threads, contamination, excessive coating, interference or locking feature.
Sudden torque dropCoating fracture, washer movement, surface collapse, slip or thread damage.
Torque rises but clamp force does notGalling, bearing ploughing, thread interference, washer deformation or bottoming.
Clamp force drops after seatingEmbedment, joint collapse, gasket compression or surface-film failure.
Jerky curveStick-slip, lubricant-film instability, intermittent tool motion or adhesion.

These curves can be used as diagnostic process fingerprints rather than reducing the test to one final friction number.

Chapter 09

Statistics, MSA & Production Control

Friction control is a manufacturing process-control problem as much as it is a laboratory problem.

Statistical friction control

A batch should not be accepted solely because the average lies within the specified range. Depending on the joint risk, acceptance may need to consider:

Central tendency

Mean and median.

Spread

Minimum, maximum, range, standard deviation and coefficient of variation.

Capability

Process capability and control-chart behaviour over time.

Individual low-friction and high-friction outliers may create assembly failures even if the overall batch average appears acceptable.

Measurement-system analysis

  • Torque calibration and traceability
  • Clamp-load sensor calibration
  • Repeatability and reproducibility
  • Alignment and fixture compliance
  • Sensor resolution and sampling frequency
  • Operator influence
  • Software calculation control
  • Temperature stability
  • Cross-laboratory correlation where required

A friction process cannot be controlled reliably if a significant portion of the observed variation comes from the measurement system itself.

Production controls

Coating process

  • Bath or coating-material condition
  • Coating thickness
  • Conversion coating
  • Sealer / topcoat concentration
  • Lubricant concentration and quantity
  • Cure temperature and time
  • Spin / centrifuge parameters
  • Post-coating handling

Fastener and verification controls

  • Thread dimensions and roughness
  • Burrs, laps and damage
  • Bearing-surface flatness
  • Washer condition and hardness
  • Cleanliness and lot traceability
  • Regular torque–tension testing
  • Control-chart monitoring
  • Calibration and retained samples
PPAP, Control Plan and change-control integration

For automotive and other controlled applications, friction validation can be integrated with drawing review, initial sample inspection, PPAP, Control Plan, Process FMEA, MSA, capability studies, supplier approval and lot traceability.

Changes to coating supplier, lubricant, sealer, topcoat, cure cycle, washer, fastener manufacturer, raw material, thread rolling, cleaning or packaging can all require revalidation. A corrosion-equivalent coating change is not necessarily friction-equivalent.

Chapter 10

Engineering Specification

A friction requirement becomes useful only when the complete tested assembly, test condition and acceptance window are defined.

What a complete specification should define

Product

Fastener standard, size, property class, material, thread tolerance and head geometry.

Coating system

Coating type, thickness, conversion coating, sealer, topcoat, lubricant and colour where applicable.

Test assembly

Mating thread, washer, bearing plate, hardness, finish and mating coating.

Test conditions

Standard, speed, temperature, conditioning, cycle count and sample count.

Acceptance limits

Thread, bearing and/or total friction range, individual limits, average limits and scatter limits.

Reporting

Individual results, averages, min/max, anomalies, test configuration and conditioning.

Recommended engineering clause

The fastener coating system shall provide controlled torque–clamp-force performance when tested in accordance with ISO 16047 using the defined production-representative mating components. Testing shall be conducted on fasteners in the as-supplied condition without additional lubrication unless expressly specified. The report shall identify the bolt or screw, mating thread, washer, bearing surface, coating system, lubricant, tightening speed, temperature, test cycle and conditioning procedure. Individual and average thread, bearing and/or total coefficient-of-friction results shall be reported together with tightening torque, clamp force and any observed abnormalities. Compliance with corrosion-resistance requirements shall not by itself constitute compliance with friction requirements.

Common specification mistakes

  • “Coefficient of friction: 0.12” without a tolerance or defined test system.
  • “Lubricated” without identifying the lubricant and application condition.
  • “Zinc flake” without identifying the topcoat / friction modifier.
  • “Torque as per standard” without defining the surface condition.
  • Using the same torque for dry and lubricated fasteners.
  • Treating K and μ as interchangeable.
  • Ignoring under-head, washer or nut-bearing friction.
  • Ignoring prevailing torque in locking systems.
  • Using a laboratory mating surface unrelated to the production joint.
  • Assuming salt-spray hours define assembly performance.
  • Accepting only an average value while ignoring scatter and outliers.
  • Reusing fasteners without validating the change in friction and locking behaviour.
Engineering principle

A coating is not complete merely because it delivers colour, thickness or corrosion resistance. It must preserve thread fit, gaugeability, drive functionality, bearing condition, torque–tension behaviour, clamp-load consistency, galling resistance, assembly repeatability and compatibility throughout storage and service.

Chapter 11

Troubleshooting & Case Studies

Torque–tension data is especially valuable when it is used to explain failures instead of merely certifying a coating batch.

Five common investigation patterns

Observed problemPossible mechanismWhat to verify
Bolts break after coating changeNew topcoat lowered friction; unchanged torque raised preload and combined stress.Compare μ, torque–clamp-force curves, coating layers and lubricant.
Gasket leaks although torque is “correct”Friction is higher than assumed; wrench reaches torque before required clamp load.Measure actual clamp force and production mating-surface friction.
Stainless threads seizeAdhesive wear / galling caused by poor lubrication, speed or material pairing.Lubricant, surface finish, hardness pairing, assembly speed and reuse.
Lab results are good; production assembly is poorLaboratory washer / speed / contamination state differs from production.Recreate the actual joint and assembly process.
Wide variation inside one lotNon-uniform lubricant, mixed batches, variable curing, thread roughness or measurement variation.Process traceability, application uniformity, MSA and lot stratification.
Failure analysis questions to ask before changing torque
  1. Did the coating, topcoat, sealer or lubricant change?
  2. Did the nut, washer or bearing surface change?
  3. Did tightening speed or tool type change?
  4. Is the fastener being reused?
  5. Is there prevailing torque or a locking patch?
  6. Is the thread damaged, oversized, contaminated or over-coated?
  7. Does the torque–angle curve show abnormal running torque or stick-slip?
  8. Does the laboratory fixture represent the actual production joint?
  9. Has storage or ageing changed the surface film?
  10. Is the measurement system capable enough to distinguish process variation from test variation?

Final design rule
The best coefficient of friction is not the lowest value. It is the value that delivers the required preload safely, consistently and repeatedly within the actual fastening system.

Technical note: friction and torque–preload values must be validated for the actual fastener, coating, lubricant, mating thread, washer, bearing surface, assembly equipment and service requirements.
