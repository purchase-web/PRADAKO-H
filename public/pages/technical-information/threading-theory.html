01 — The helix

Pitch, lead and the angle everything depends on

Unroll one turn of a thread and you get a right triangle. Its base is the circumference at the pitch diameter; its height is the lead. The angle between them is the helix angle, and it decides how much of your input torque becomes clamp load, and whether the joint stays tight when you let go of the spanner.

P

Pitch

Axial distance from one thread crest to the next. The single dimension that defines the whole profile — every other basic diameter is derived from it.

L

Lead

Axial travel in one full turn. L = n × P, where n is the number of starts. On the single-start threads used for fasteners, lead and pitch are identical.

n

Starts

Independent helices cut side by side. Multi-start threads travel further per turn at the same profile depth — fast to actuate, but they lose self-locking.

λ

Helix angle

The lead angle measured at the pitch diameter. For ISO metric coarse threads it sits between roughly 1.8° and 4.6° — small, and deliberately so.

tan λ = L / (π · d₂)  =  n · P / (π · d₂)

λ helix (lead) angle at the pitch diameter  ·  L lead, mm  ·  P pitch, mm  ·  n number of starts  ·  d₂ pitch diameter, mm

Why the angle is kept small

A fastener is a screw jack you want to stay jacked. The thread stays put under static load when the friction angle at the flanks exceeds the helix angle — so a shallow helix is what makes a bolt a fastener rather than a lead screw. Fine-pitch threads have a smaller λ than coarse, which is why they hold marginally better under vibration and give finer preload control per degree of rotation.

Self-locking when   λ < arctan( μ / cos(α/2) )

μ coefficient of friction at the thread flanks  ·  α included thread angle (60° for ISO metric)
With μ = 0.12 and α = 60°, the limiting angle is about 7.9° — comfortably above the 1.8–4.6° range of ISO metric coarse threads. Self-locking under static load is therefore inherent. It is not a defence against transverse vibration; see §10.

4.64°

Steepest helix in the ISO metric coarse series — M1.6 × 0.35.

1.82°

Shallowest in the coarse series — M64 × 6. Large threads are proportionally flatter.

7.9° limit

Self-locking threshold at μ = 0.12. Every standard fastener thread sits well below it.

Note on fine pitch

A fine thread has a larger stress area than a coarse thread of the same nominal diameter — M10 × 1 carries about 11% more section than M10 × 1.5. It also has a shallower helix and finer preload resolution. What it loses is tolerance to damage, speed of assembly, and depth of engagement per turn in soft or tapped materials. Coarse remains the default for general assembly; fine earns its place in thin-walled sections, adjustment threads, and vibration-exposed joints.

03 — Thread form families

Different jobs, different triangles

The 60° symmetric profile is optimised for clamping. Change the job — sealing, power transmission, one-directional thrust, forming its own mating thread — and the profile changes with it.

60° SYMMETRIC

ISO metric — M

The world default for clamping fasteners. Flat crest, rounded root, equal flank angles. Coarse and fine series under one profile definition.

  • Standard ISO 68-1 / 261 / 262
  • Angle 60° included
  • Use General clamped joints

60° · FLAT CREST

Unified — UNC / UNF / UNEF

Inch equivalent of ISO metric, sharing the 60° angle but specified in threads per inch with its own tolerance classes. Not interchangeable with metric.

  • Standard ASME B1.1
  • Classes 1A/2A/3A · 1B/2B/3B
  • Use North American assemblies

55° ROUNDED

Whitworth — BSW / BSF

Rounded crests and roots on a 55° angle. Largely superseded for new design, but essential for legacy plant, heritage rail and repair work.

  • Standard BS 84
  • Angle 55° included
  • Use Legacy & restoration

55° TAPER 1:16

Pipe — BSPT / NPT

Taper threads that seal on the flanks themselves as they wedge. Parallel variants (BSPP) seal on a face or washer instead.

  • Standard ISO 7-1 · ASME B1.20.1
  • Taper 1 in 16 on diameter
  • Use Pressure-tight joints

30° TRAPEZOIDAL

Trapezoidal — Tr

Broad flat flanks carry high axial load with low flank pressure. The metric power thread — presses, jacks, lead screws, clamping devices.

  • Angle 30° included
  • Use Power transmission

Acme / Stub Acme

The inch power thread. Slightly steeper flanks than trapezoidal, with general-purpose and centralising classes. Stub Acme uses a reduced depth.

One near-vertical load flank and one steep clearance flank. Carries very high thrust in a single direction with minimal radial bursting force.

  • Angle 7° load / 45° clearance
  • Use Presses, breech rings

30–48° · DEEP, SHARP

Tapping & thread-forming

Deep, sharp, widely spaced threads that cut or cold-form their own mating thread in sheet, casting or plastic. Trilobular forms reduce driving torque.

04 — Tolerance, allowance and fit

Nothing is made to basic size

The basic profile is a theoretical reference. Real threads are displaced away from it and given a tolerance band. In ISO 965 the displacement is a letter — the fundamental deviation — and the band width is a number, the tolerance grade. Together they make a tolerance class such as 6g or 6H.

LETTER

Fundamental deviation — where the band sits

External threads use lower-case: e, f, g, h. Internal use upper-case: G, H. Position h and H sit exactly on basic size, with zero allowance. The other positions pull material away from the mating part to leave room for coating, for hot-dip galvanizing, or simply for assembly clearance.

NUMBER

Tolerance grade — how wide the band is

Grades run 3 to 9; larger number, wider band. Grade 6 is the medium reference, equivalent in intent to a general commercial fit. Grades 3–5 are fine, 7–9 coarse. A class may carry two grades — 5g6g means grade 5 on the pitch diameter and grade 6 on the major diameter.

es upper deviation of the external thread  ·  EI lower deviation of the internal thread  ·  P pitch in mm. The deviation applies equally to the major, pitch and minor diameters.

Reading a class designation

M10 × 1.5 – 6g is an external thread, grade 6, position g. M10 × 1.5 – 6H is the mating nut. Together they form the fit written as 6H/6g — the general-purpose combination that covers the overwhelming majority of commercial fasteners. Where a class is written twice, as in 5g6g, the first pair applies to the pitch diameter and the second to the crest diameter.

FINE

4H / 4h — 5H / 5h6h

Close fits for precision assemblies, adjustment threads and instruments. Requires controlled process capability and gauging discipline.

MEDIUM

6H / 6g

The commercial default. Assumes a normal length of engagement and permits a light electroplated coating within the g allowance.

COARSE

7H / 8g

Hot-dip galvanized work, hot-formed threads, structural assemblies and anywhere contamination or thick coating must be tolerated.

Coating allowance — the practical rule

A coating of thickness t deposited on both flanks increases the pitch diameter by approximately 4 t — the flanks are inclined at 30° to the axis, so radial build-up is amplified. A 8 µm zinc plate therefore consumes about 32 µm of pitch diameter. Compare that against the g allowance for the pitch: at P = 1.5, es(g) is −31.5 µm, so 8 µm of plating uses essentially the whole allowance. Heavier coatings — hot-dip galvanizing at 45–85 µm — require the nut to be tapped oversize per ISO 10684 rather than relying on the bolt tolerance class.

A BOLT DOES NOT CARRY LOAD ON ITS NOMINAL DIAMETER.

It carries load on the tensile stress area — an effective section lying between the pitch diameter and the root. Design against the nominal and you will overestimate capacity by roughly a quarter.

05 — Tensile stress area and strength

The effective section

A threaded bar loaded in tension does not fail at the minor diameter, as a plain notched bar would, and it does not carry the full pitch-diameter section either. ISO 898-1 resolves this with an empirical effective diameter — the mean of the pitch diameter and the rounded-root minor diameter.

Aₛ = (π / 4) · [ (d₂ + d₃) / 2 ]² where  d₃ = d₁ − H/6  =  d − 1.226869 · P

Aₛ nominal tensile stress area, mm²  ·  d₂ basic pitch diameter  ·  d₃ minor diameter of the external thread at the rounded root.
For M10 × 1.5: d₂ = 9.026, d₃ = 8.160, mean = 8.593, Aₛ = 58.0 mm². The nominal-diameter area would read 78.5 mm² — an overestimate of 35%.

Match the nut to the bolt

ISO 898-2 nut property classes are numbered to match: a class 8 nut is proof-loaded against a class 8.8 bolt, class 10 against 10.9, class 12 against 12.9. The intent of the pairing is that the bolt breaks in tension before the threads strip — a visible, diagnosable failure rather than a silent loss of clamp. Fitting a class 8 nut on a 12.9 screw inverts that logic and puts the failure where you cannot see it.

06 — Length of engagement

How deep is deep enough

Thread stripping is a shear failure across a helical surface. Its capacity grows with engaged length; the bolt's tensile capacity does not. There is therefore a minimum engagement above which the bolt is guaranteed to be the weaker member — which is exactly where you want the failure to occur.

Ath,n = π · d₁ · (LE / P) · [ P/2 + (d₂ − D₁) · tan(30°) ]  — nut thread shear Ath,b = π · D₁ · (LE / P) · [ P/2 + (d₂ − D₁) · tan(30°) ]  — bolt thread shear

LE length of engagement  ·  d₁ external minor diameter  ·  D₁ internal minor diameter. Stripping strength is then the shear area multiplied by the shear strength of the weaker material, conventionally taken as 0.6 × its ultimate tensile strength.

The material strength ratio

When bolt and tapped component are different materials, the required engagement scales with their strength ratio. A steel screw into aluminium needs roughly twice the engagement of the same screw into steel; into a magnesium or zinc casting, more again. The practical starting points below assume a class 8.8 screw and full-form threads.

1.0× d

Steel into steel. One nominal diameter of engagement, the basis of standard nut heights, which are typically 0.8 d.

1.5× d

Into cast iron. Allows for the lower shear strength and the risk of a partially formed first thread at the surface.

2.0× d

Into aluminium alloy. The most common engagement failure in production — specified on nominal length rather than usable thread depth.

2.5× d

Into magnesium, zinc and reinforced polymer. Consider a threaded insert instead where the joint will be repeatedly disassembled.

Short, normal and long — the S / N / L groups

ISO 965-1 recognises that tolerance requirements change with engagement length, and divides them into three groups. Group N — normal — spans roughly 2.24 P · d0.2 to 6.7 P · d0.2 and covers ordinary nut-and-bolt assembly; a tolerance class quoted without a group letter is understood to be N. Group S covers engagements shorter than that band and generally warrants a finer grade, because there is less thread available to average out pitch and flank-angle error. Group L covers longer engagements, where accumulated pitch deviation over many threads makes a coarser grade the realistic choice.

Count usable threads, not hole depth

A tapped hole loses thread at both ends: an incomplete first thread from the chamfer, and an unusable run-out at the bottom of a blind hole. Both are real, and both are routinely left out of the drawing note. Specify minimum full-form thread depth and let the driller work backwards from it, rather than dimensioning the drilled depth and hoping.

07 — Torque, friction and preload

Most of your torque never becomes clamp load

Tightening torque is spent in three places: overcoming friction under the bearing face, overcoming friction on the thread flanks, and doing the useful work of stretching the bolt. In a typical dry steel joint the split is roughly 50 / 40 / 10 — only about a tenth of the applied torque generates preload.

T = F · [ 0.16 · P + 0.58 · μth · d₂ + μb · DKm / 2 ]

T tightening torque, N·mm  ·  F preload, N  ·  P pitch  ·  μth thread friction coefficient  ·  μb bearing-face friction coefficient  ·  d₂ pitch diameter  ·  DKm effective mean bearing diameter under the head or nut.
The 0.16 P term is the pure helix work; 0.58 is 1 / (2 cos 30°), converting flank friction to the axial direction.

T ≈ K · d · F  — the short form

K nut factor, dimensionless. Convenient, but it is an empirical lumped constant, not a material property: it changes with coating, lubricant, surface finish, speed and the number of times the joint has been used. Use it for a first estimate and verify by test. Typical values: 0.20 plain steel dry · 0.18 zinc plated · 0.14 waxed or lubricated · 0.12 PTFE coated · 0.10 molybdenum disulphide.

Torque control

±25–35% preload scatter

The universal method, because it needs nothing but a wrench. Its weakness is that it measures the sum of three quantities and infers the smallest one. A change in friction the operator cannot see moves the resulting preload by a third.

  • Cheap, fast, auditable
  • Highly sensitive to lubrication state
  • Requires a specified, controlled surface condition to be meaningful
Angle & yield control

±10% or better

Snug to a defined torque, then rotate a specified angle. Past snug, rotation maps almost linearly onto bolt extension, and extension maps onto preload — friction largely drops out of the measurement.

  • Torque-plus-angle is the automotive standard for critical joints
  • Yield control detects the knee in the torque–angle curve directly
  • Bolt tension by ultrasonic length or load-indicating washer is better still

Coarse or fine for preload control? Fine threads develop more preload per unit torque, because the 0.16 P helix term shrinks while the friction terms barely move. That makes them useful where torque capacity is limited — but it also makes them less forgiving of over-torque, since the same wrench error travels further up the load curve.

HOW THE THREAD IS MADE CHANGES HOW LONG IT LIVES.

Two threads to the same drawing, one cut and one rolled, are not the same component. Grain flow, root compressive stress and surface finish differ — and so does fatigue life, by a factor that is not marginal.

08 — How threads are made

Rolled, cut, ground and formed

Cutting removes metal to leave a thread. Rolling displaces metal to raise one. The distinction is not a manufacturing preference — it determines where the grain runs, what residual stress sits in the root, and how the part behaves under cyclic load.

Cold rolled

Displaced, not removed

The blank is rolled between hardened dies carrying the negative profile. Metal flows from what becomes the root into what becomes the crest. Grain follows the profile continuously around the root radius, and the cold work leaves compressive residual stress exactly where fatigue cracks would otherwise initiate.

  • Uninterrupted grain flow around the root
  • Compressive residual stress at the fatigue-critical radius
  • Work-hardened, burnished flank surface
  • No swarf; blank diameter close to pitch diameter
  • Substantially faster in volume production
Cut or ground

Severed grain

Single-point turning, die-cutting, tapping or thread grinding remove material to the finished profile. Grain lines are cut through where they meet the flank, and the machined root carries tool marks. Indispensable for large diameters, short runs, hardened material and internal threads — but it starts from a weaker fatigue baseline.

  • Grain terminated at the flank surface
  • Neutral or tensile residual stress in the root
  • Surface finish set by tool condition
  • Free choice of pitch and form without dedicated tooling
  • The only practical route for most internal threads

Roll before or after heat treatment?

Rolling before quenching and tempering is the standard route — the blank is soft, die life is long, and it suits volume production. Rolling after heat treatment retains the compressive root stress in the finished part instead of relieving it in the furnace, and is the recognised route to the highest fatigue performance in high strength classes. It costs more in die wear and is worth specifying only where cyclic load, not static strength, sets the design.

Rolling process routes

  1. Flat die rolling

    One fixed and one reciprocating die. The blank rolls between them and leaves fully threaded in a single pass. The highest-volume route for standard fastener sizes, and the cheapest per piece once set.

  2. Cylindrical die rolling — two or three roll

    Rotating dies close radially on the blank. Handles longer parts, larger diameters and threads placed away from the end, and gives closer control of the finished pitch diameter.

  3. Planetary rolling

    A rotating centre die inside a fixed segment feeds parts continuously. Very high throughput on small-to-medium screws where cycle time dominates cost.

  4. Thread rolling attachment

    Axial or tangential heads mounted on a lathe or screw machine, rolling the thread as part of the turning cycle. Bridges the gap between machining and dedicated rolling.

Blank diameter and tapping allowance

Rolling blank ≈ d₂ + (0.1 to 0.3) · (d − d₂)  — material dependent Tap drill ≈ d − P  → approximately 76% thread engagement Engagement % = (d − Ddrill) / (1.0825 · P) × 100

The rolling blank is close to, but slightly above, the pitch diameter — the exact figure depends on how much the material work-hardens and flows. Blank diameter is the single most important control variable in thread rolling and the usual root cause of oversize or undersize pitch diameter.

On tapping: going from 65% to 75% engagement adds roughly 5% to the stripping strength but can double the tapping torque. Higher engagement is not automatically better — 75% is the practical optimum for steel, and 60–70% is often the right answer in tough or gummy materials where tap breakage is the real risk.

09 — Fatigue and failure modes

Where threads actually break

Load does not distribute evenly along an engaged thread. The first engaged thread — the one at the nut bearing face — takes something like a third of the total, and each thread beyond it takes progressively less. Past about six engaged threads the additional ones contribute almost nothing. That is why nuts do not get taller indefinitely, and why the first thread root is where cracks start.

First-thread root crack

Fatigue initiation at the root of the first engaged thread, where both stress concentration and load share peak simultaneously.

→ Rolled root · larger root radius · tapered or flanged nut

Thread stripping

Shear failure of the engaged flanks. Silent, progressive, and it removes clamp load without any external sign that the joint has failed.

→ Engagement length · matched property classes · check tapped depth

Run-out fracture

Failure at the thread run-out or the head-to-shank fillet — the two other stress raisers competing with the first engaged thread.

→ Generous under-head radius · avoid run-out in the clamped length

Hydrogen embrittlement

Delayed brittle fracture in high-strength classes, hours or days after installation. Hydrogen from pickling or electroplating diffuses to the stressed root.

→ Bake within 4 h of plating · consider mechanical zinc or Zn-flake

The counter-intuitive one: high preload improves fatigue life

In a properly designed joint, a fluctuating external load is largely absorbed by the clamped members, not by the bolt. The higher the preload, the smaller the share of the cyclic load that reaches the bolt, and the lower its stress amplitude. Under-tightening is therefore a fatigue problem, not a conservative safety margin — a loose bolt in a cyclically loaded joint sees the full alternating load and fails long before a correctly tensioned one.

10 — Locking and vibration

Two different failures, often confused

A joint can lose clamp load in two entirely different ways. In rotational loosening, the nut actually turns. In non-rotational loss, it does not — the preload disappears through embedding, gasket creep, or relaxation of the clamped members. Fitting a locking device to a joint suffering the second problem accomplishes nothing.

Rotational loosening

The nut turns

Transverse vibration is the dominant cause. Cyclic side load makes the joint faces slip microscopically; during slip, thread and bearing friction fall to near zero, and the helix — which is a wedge under axial load — drives rotation. Self-locking under static load offers no protection at all against this mechanism.

  • Reproduced by the Junker transverse vibration test, DIN 65151
  • Answered by prevailing torque, adhesive or geometric locking
  • Anything that prevents slip in the first place also solves it
Non-rotational loss

The nut stays put

Surface asperities flatten under load — embedding — typically consuming a few micrometres per interface. Gaskets creep. Coatings compress. In a short-grip joint those few micrometres can be a large fraction of the total bolt extension, and therefore a large fraction of the preload.

  • Worst in short grip lengths, soft coatings and multi-interface stacks
  • Answered by longer grip, fewer interfaces, harder surfaces, re-torque
  • A locking device does not address it
TYPE 01

Prevailing torque

Nylon insert or deformed thread. Resistance to rotation independent of clamp load, so it still functions if preload is partly lost. Temperature-limited for nylon, and reusability is finite.

TYPE 02

Chemical locking

Anaerobic adhesive, either applied at assembly or pre-applied as a patch or microencapsulated coating. Fills the clearance and prevents slip. Strength grade must match the intended service and disassembly needs.

TYPE 03

Geometric wedge locking

Paired washers with cam faces steeper than the thread helix, so any attempted rotation must lift the bolt against its own preload. Effective under severe transverse vibration; requires correct pairing and orientation.

TYPE 04

Positive mechanical locking

Split pins, castellated nuts, lock wire, tab washers. Cannot rotate more than a defined amount regardless of preload. The default where a loosening event is unacceptable — aerospace and defence practice.

On split ring lock washers

The helical spring washer is the most widely fitted and least effective locking device in general use. Under any realistic preload it flattens solid and behaves as a plain washer; controlled transverse vibration testing has repeatedly shown it to give little or no benefit, and in some configurations to loosen faster than a plain washer by introducing an extra interface to embed. It survives on habit. If a joint genuinely needs locking, choose from the four types above and qualify the choice by test.

Removes the material

The pilot hole is the specification

Every tapping screw has a narrow band of correct hole size. Too small and driving torque climbs until the screw shears or the boss splits; too large and stripping torque falls until the joint has no holding power. The number that matters is the ratio of stripping torque to driving torque — aim for at least 2:1 in a hand-assembled joint and higher where automated drivers with torque scatter are used. 13 — Self-drilling screw threads

Drill, tap and fasten in one motion

A self-drilling screw carries its own twist-drill point. It must complete three operations in sequence during a single driver cycle — drill through, form the thread, then seat and clamp — and each has to finish before the next begins. Get the sequence wrong and the screw either burns out its point or strips the hole it just made.

The three failures

  1. Point burn-out

    Drilling continues past the point's thermal limit because the driver speed is too high or the steel too thick for the point class. The tip work-hardens the hole and stops cutting. Correct by dropping to a lower driver speed, not by pushing harder.

  2. Premature thread engagement

    The first thread reaches the top sheet before the drill point has broken through the bottom one. The screw then tries to pull itself through material it has not yet drilled, and either shears or lifts the sheets apart. This is a flute-length selection error and the most common self-drilling fault on site.

  3. Over-drive and strip

    The driver keeps turning after seating. Thin-gauge steel strips at the formed thread almost instantly. Depth-sensing nose cones and clutch settings exist for exactly this reason.

14 — Threads for thermoplastics

Narrow flank angles, because plastic bursts

Driving a screw into a moulded boss generates radial pressure. A conventional 60° profile directs a large part of the forming force outwards, and the boss cracks — sometimes immediately, more often weeks later under thermal cycling. The answer is a narrower flank angle, a deeper thread and a smaller core.

The families

PT — 30° symmetric, wide pitch

The reference thread form for thermoplastic assembly. A 30° flank angle cuts radial pressure to roughly half that of a 60° profile, and a widely spaced, deep thread on a slim core gives the displaced polymer somewhere to flow. The practical result is a wider torque window — a bigger gap between the torque needed to drive and the torque that strips — which is what makes automated assembly repeatable.

Delta PT and asymmetric derivatives

Refinements of the PT concept with modified root radii and asymmetric flanks that direct polymer flow towards the core rather than outwards. Intended for glass-filled, mineral-filled and higher-modulus engineering resins where plain PT begins to crack the boss.

Y-type and 45° forming threads

A narrow-flank asymmetric family used where the resin is stiff enough to resist a 30° form but too brittle for 60°. These are proprietary geometries rather than ISO- or DIN-defined forms, so they are made and gauged to customer drawing — Pradako holds the tooling for the profiles it manufactures and controls them against the customer's own print, not against a public standard.

15 — High-Low thread

Two thread heights on one helix

The High-Low form alternates a tall, sharp 30° thread with a short intermediate one on the same lead. The high thread cuts deep into low-density material to generate shear area; the low thread sits between, supporting the formed material and stopping it from collapsing back. The core diameter is smaller than a conventional screw of the same nominal size, so there is more material engaged and less displaced.

Why it works in low-density material

In plastic, timber, chipboard and drywall the limit is never the screw — it is the shear strength of the surrounding material. High-Low attacks that directly: the tall sharp thread increases the axial shear area engaged per unit length, while the 30° included angle keeps radial bursting pressure to roughly half that of a conventional 60° form. That combination is what allows a smaller boss, a fixing closer to a board edge, and a usable thread in brittle phenolic or glass-filled material.

16 — Double-start and twin-lead threads

Two helices, twice the travel per turn

Cut a second thread starting 180° round from the first, at the same pitch, and the lead doubles while the profile depth stays the same. The screw drives in half the revolutions. Section 01 already gives the consequence: the helix angle roughly doubles too, and the thread moves closer to the self-locking limit.

17 — Wood, chipboard and decking threads

A thread that must not split the material it holds

Timber fails by splitting along the grain, and the splitting force is the radial component the thread generates as it drives. Every feature of a modern wood screw exists to reduce that component while keeping shear area high: a sharp narrow thread, a slim core, a partially unthreaded shank, and a cutting slot at the tip.

POINT

Type 17 cut

A slot milled through the tip turns the point into a cutting edge with a chip pocket. Starts without a pilot hole and dramatically reduces edge splitting.

23 — Gauging and measurement

Functional size, and why GO is not the opposite of NO-GO

A thread has more independent errors than it has tolerances. Pitch diameter, lead, flank angle and roundness can each be wrong on their own — but the pitch-diameter tolerance has to absorb all of them. That single fact is the basis of Taylor's principle and of every gauge on the shop floor.

Functional size  =  simple pitch diameter  +  lead error  +  flank angle error δlead = 1.732 · |Δp|  ·  δangle ≈ 0.0115 · P · (|Δα₁| + |Δα₂|)

Δp cumulative lead deviation over the length of engagement, mm  ·  Δα flank half-angle deviation, in minutes of arc  ·  P pitch, mm.
Both errors consume pitch-diameter tolerance without ever appearing on a pitch-micrometer reading. A thread can measure perfectly on simple pitch diameter and still fail a GO ring.

GO gauge

Checks maximum material — full form, full length

The GO gauge carries the complete thread profile over a length at least equal to the length of engagement. It must assemble freely. Because it engages the whole form over the whole length, it checks all the errors together — pitch diameter, lead, flank angle and straightness in one operation.

  • Must enter and pass the full thread length under its own weight or light hand force
  • Verifies functional size, not simple pitch diameter
  • Wear is inward on a ring, outward on a plug — a worn GO passes bad work
NO-GO gauge

Checks minimum material — truncated, short

The NO-GO is deliberately truncated on crest and root and only two or three threads long, so that it responds to pitch diameter alone and not to lead or flank-angle error. It is permitted to enter — the acceptance criterion is a limited number of turns, not zero entry.

  • Entry of up to about three turns is normally acceptable on external threads
  • Truncated form deliberately blind to lead and angle error
  • A NO-GO that will not start at all can indicate an undersize thread, not a good one

Taylor's principle, stated properly

The GO gauge checks the maximum material condition of all dimensions simultaneously; the NO-GO gauge checks the minimum material condition of one dimension at a time. Every correct thread gauge design follows from that sentence. It is also why a NO-GO ring is short and truncated rather than simply being a GO ring cut to a different size — a full-form NO-GO would reject good parts on lead error that the tolerance was never meant to catch twice.

Three-wire measurement

The reference method for simple pitch diameter on an external thread. Three precision wires are seated in the thread grooves — one on one side, two on the other — and the distance over the wires is measured with a micrometer. Pitch diameter follows from the geometry of the wire sitting on the flanks.

24 — Tapping the internal thread

Cutting taps and forming taps need different holes

This is the single most common production error in internal threading. A forming tap displaces material rather than removing it, so the hole must be larger — drilled to the pitch-diameter region rather than to the minor diameter. Use a cutting-tap drill chart on a forming tap and the tap breaks.

Cutting tap

Removes a chip

Fluted, with cutting edges that sever material and flutes that evacuate it. Works in almost any machinable material including cast iron and free-cutting steels, and is the only option in brittle or non-ductile material.

  • Drill ≈ d − P for about 76% thread
  • Produces swarf that must be cleared, especially in blind holes
  • Lower torque than forming, but a weaker, non-work-hardened thread
  • Spiral point for through holes, spiral flute for blind
Forming tap

Displaces material

Lobed and fluteless. It cold-forms the thread, work-hardening the surface and producing an uninterrupted grain flow. Stronger thread, no chips, longer tool life — but it needs a ductile material and a materially different hole size.

  • Drill ≈ d − 0.5 P for about 65% thread
  • No swarf — correct for blind holes and sealed assemblies
  • Higher torque; unsuitable for cast iron and brittle material
  • Thread stronger than the parent material at the surface
Cutting tap:   Ddrill = d − ( %E / 76.98 ) · P Forming tap:   Ddrill = d − ( %E / 100 ) · 1.082532 · P · 0.5 Engagement achieved:   %E = ( d − Ddrill ) / ( 1.082532 · P ) × 100

%E percentage of full thread depth engaged  ·  d nominal diameter  ·  P pitch.
Going from 65% to 75% engagement adds roughly 5% to stripping strength but can double tapping torque. Higher engagement is not automatically better. 70–75% is the practical optimum in steel; 60–65% is often correct in tough, gummy or thin-walled work where tap breakage is the real risk.

25 — Galling and cold welding

The stainless failure that seizes mid-assembly

Stainless steel protects itself with a passive chromium oxide film a few nanometres thick. Thread flanks sliding under load rupture that film, exposing clean metal to clean metal at high local pressure. The two surfaces cold-weld, tear, and the joint locks solid — often with the nut half-way down and the assembly scrap.

A2 / A4Austenitic grades are the most galling-prone in common use
Work-hardenAustenitic steel hardens as it tears, accelerating the seizure
SecondsOnset to lock-up is typically a fraction of one assembly cycle
ScrapA galled joint usually destroys both the bolt and the tapped part
CAUSE 01

Speed

Power-driving generates frictional heat faster than it can dissipate. Run stainless threads slowly — hand assembly or a low-speed driver is a legitimate specification.

CAUSE 02

Identical alloys

Like against like galls worst. Pairing dissimilar grades or hardnesses — for example an A4 bolt with a harder nut — interrupts the cold-welding mechanism.

CAUSE 03

Surface and debris

Rough flanks, burrs, damaged crests and grit all concentrate contact pressure. Rolled threads gall less than cut threads for exactly this reason.

What actually works

In order of effectiveness: an anti-seize compound or dry-film lubricant applied to the thread; a coating such as PTFE, molybdenum disulphide or a zinc-flake system that separates the surfaces; dissimilar mating grades or hardnesses; slow, steady driving without interruption once started; and rolled rather than cut threads for their burnished flank finish. Note that any lubricant changes the nut factor substantially — if torque control is in use, the torque figure must be re-derived for the lubricated condition, not carried over from the dry specification.
